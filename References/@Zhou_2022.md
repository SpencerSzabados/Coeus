# Overview
The authors develop a improved variant of the Legendre Memory Unit ([[@Volker_2019]], [[@Chilkuri_2021]]) for performing long-horizon forecasts. This model achieves at considerable $20\%$ improvement over state-of-the-art models (ML models). 

# Primary results 
It is argued by the authors that increased model complexity, for LSTM and original Transformer architecture, to accurately forecast data at the model will being overfitting the noise present in the data and lose the ability to make accurate fine grained predictions. The two key aspects of forming an accurate prediction are then stated by the authors to be 
  1) Accurately capturing historical information;
  2) Effectively removing noise present in training data;
meaning any model that capable of providing accurate long range forecasts must be inherently robust to a large amount of noise in training data. Consequently, the problem of long-term time-series forecasting can be studied (in the sense of model comparison) by long-term sequence comparison accuracy (models ability to recall information accurately over long sequences). 

Based on experimentation the authors state that LMU models, while accurate in recovering data, are sensitive to noise and consequently are not guaranteed to perform well in long-term sequence comparisons due to overfitting. The authors address this issue by  
  1) A data normalization layer is inserted in front of the Legendre projection layer; specifically, a network called RevIN is used. 
  2) Dynamically updating the time-series representation using fixed-sized vectors; where the window size $\theta$ in the projection layer is fixed to be not _too large_ as longer histories of data may accumulate more noise in the polynomial fitment. 
  3) To reduce the impact of noise in the data on the Legendre projection a dimension reduction layer is introduced following the Legendre projection layer in the form of a Fourier transformation based approximation using a low-rank matrix and random sampling over the dimensions using a spectral basis (in frequency domain due to Fourier transformation). The output of this layer is then passed to a final projection layer that is responsible for reconstructing (recalling) the data.
  4) Lastly another data normalization layer is placed before the output; the same RevIN model is used here again.
The layers composing (2)-to-(3) are then stacked with different time horizons $\{T,2T,\dots,nT\}$  and combined using a linear layer improve forecasting performance for a desired forecasting horizon $T$.
