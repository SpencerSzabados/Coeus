# Overview
This paper formalizes the mismatch present (at the time of writing) between the theory underpinning score matching [[Diffusion models]] and the common implementations of these methods including several empirically motivated adjustments that are often used to achieve state-of-the-art performance on image benchmarks. A key point of discussion is whether the common practice of projecting data back into the pixel domain by thresholding values to the allowed range after each pass through the diffusion process (many passes are often necessary to generate high quality images when using standard U-Net architectures), which is done in an attempt to counteract accumulated numerical error, is justifiable or if it represents a sufficient departure from the underlying theory. This question is studied by developing a _reflected diffusion model_ that constrains the diffusion process to remain on a bounded domain that is assumed to contain the support of the data (which is selected beforehand - either as the unit hypercube or corner of cube simplex), and analysing what form of operation applied to this diffusion process corresponds to thresholding (projecting) data back to pixel space in "ordinary" diffusion; this concludes in a theorem that, in the limit of the number of steps performed at each diffusion pass, states thresholding is a suitable approximation the _true underlying_ reflected diffusion process.

The cost of performing reflected diffusion compared to unreflect diffusion is analysed and shown to not be substantially more costly, a near constant factor increase per step needed to generate the appropriate constrained (reflected) noise. With that said, according to the authors, as the computational cost of performing reflection operations is "negligible" in the face the cost of neural network computations, the training and inferences costs are more or less equivalent to other methods.

# Primary results
Reflected diffusion models are defined over a data domain $\Omega$, taken to be connected and compact with nonempty interior and uniform Hausdorff dimension, which is (constructed) assumed to outer-bound the support $\mathcal{X}$ of the data distribution $p$.

Throughout all experiments $\Omega$ is taken to be either the unit hypercube $$H^d = \{x\in\mathbb{R}^d \mid 0\leq x_i\leq 1\}$$or the projected corn-of-cube simplex $$\Delta^d = \left\{x\in\mathbb{}R^d\mid \sum_{i=1}^{d-1}x_i\leq1, x_i\geq 0\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^d.$$
Reflected diffusion incorporates an additional $L_t$ term in the stochastic differential diffusion equations used in [[Diffusion models]] which reflects the diffusion trajectory according to the normal direction of the boundary. In particular, an initial starting point $X_0\sim p$ evolves according to $$dX(t) = \mu(X(t),t)dt+\sigma(t)dW(t)+dL(t),$$where $L(t)$ is equal to zero unless $X(t)$ is on the boundary $\partial\Omega$ in which case $L(t)$ equals the negative of the outwards normal component of $X(t)$ thereby ensuring $X(t)$ remains within $\Omega$; more, specifically, per the reference cited by the authors, [[@Pilipenko_2014]], $L(t)$ (TODO - finish notes.)

Likewise, in terms of probabilities, $p$ evolves according to the [[Diffusion models#Forwards diffusion equation|(forwards) Fokker-Planck equation]] with Neumann boundary conditions: $$\begin{align*}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p(x,t) = \nabla_x\cdot(-p(x,t)\mu(x,t)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(t)\nabla_xp(x,t)\\ \text{subject to }(p(x,t)\mu(x,t)-\sigma^2(t)\nabla_xp(x,t))\cdot n(x)=0 \text{ if }x\in\partial\Omega, \end{align*}$$where $n(x)$ is the inwards facing normal vector along the boundary $\partial\Omega$ at $x$.

The corresponding reverse processes is $$dX(t) = [\mu(X(t),t)-\sigma^2(t)\nabla_x\log(p(X(t),t))]dt+\sigma(t)d\overleftarrow{W}(t)+d\overleftarrow{L}(t)$$where $\overleftarrow{W}(t)$ is the reversed Brownian monition process, and $\overleftarrow{L}(t)$ is the "reversed boundary condition" (TODO - meaning what?).

The reflected SDE has a unique strong solution provided $mu$ and $\sigma$ are Lipschitz in both sate and time (indecently or jointly?) and $\Omega$ satisfies the _uniform exterior sphere condition_ presented in [Pilipenko 2014].


## Score matching method
In implementation, the diffusion variances $\sigma$ is chosen to follow the _Reflected Variance Exploding_ schedule, where $\sigma_0<<\sigma_1$ and for $t\in [0,1]$ the general equation is$$\sigma(t)=\sigma_0^{1-t}\sigma_1^t\sqrt{2\log(\sigma_1/\sigma_0)},$$and $\mu$ is taken to be identically zero.

Standard (implicit) score matching does not apply neatly to learning the score function on bounded domains, thus the authors necessarily developed a alternative objective function called  _Constrained denoising score matching (CDSM)_ based on denoising score matching: let $p(x,0)$ be the initial distribution and $p(x,t)$ be that perturbed by the diffusion process through convolution with gaussian noise on $\Omega$, define the CDSM loss as $$E^{\Omega}_{t,X_0\sim p(x,0),X(t)\sim p(x,t)}\lambda(t)\|s_\theta(X(t),t)-\nabla_x\log(p(X(t),t|X_0))\|_2^2,$$which is an expectation restricted to the support $\Omega$.

In practice, the authors set $\lambda(t)\propto \sigma^2$ and attempt to minimize variance during the optimization steps.

**Theorem: (CDSM)** Perturbing an initial probability density $p(x)$ supported on $\Omega$ by another density (noise) $w(x|\cdot)$ also supported on $\Omega$ results in a new density $p'(x)=\int_\Omega p(y)w(x|y)\,dy$. Provided $p$ and $w$ are sufficiently smooth (i.e., near $\partial\Omega$) the score matching loss for $p'$, defined as: $$\frac{1}{2}E^\Omega_{x\sim p'}\|s_\theta(x)-\nabla_x\log(p'(x))\|_2^2$$is equal up to a constant factor (independent of the quality of the learned score matching model $s_\theta$) to the CSDM loss $$\frac{1}{2}E^\Omega_{X_0\sim p(x)}E^\Omega_{X\sim w(\cdot|x_0)}\|s_\theta-\nabla_x\log(p'(X|X_0))\|_2^2.$$
 

### Refection sampling 
To train the model we need to be able to sample from $p(x_t,t|x_0)$. This is done by following a random walk (e.g., [[Markov chains|Markov chain]], a method similar to that performed in [[@Kalai_2006]]) that reflects at the boundary of $\Omega$; in particular, given $x_0$ we draw a sample $y\sim \mathcal{N}(x_0,\frac{\sigma^2_t}{2} I)$, and then iteratively follow the polyline path within $\Omega$ that results from reflecting, in the direction of the normal vector of the boundary $\partial\Omega$, the line segment $ty+(1-t)x_0$, for $t$ from $0$ to $1$, when it crosses the boundary $\partial\Omega$. 

According to the authors this process quickly converges and is easy to compute using known [[Computational geometry]] techniques.


### Score estimation (computation)
Two methods are combined in order to efficiently (e.g., in practice they can limit the number of iterative updates to approximately five) compute the score at any sample value; note, this is all formulated under the assumption that $\Omega$ is the Hypercube $H^d$.

The proposed hybrid algorithm for estimating the score takes $O(kd)$ time, opposed to $O(d)$ for regular score matching, for some _small_ $k$ (e.g., $k\approx 5$).


# Evaluating the model (generating images)
The proposed model consists of a score (model) $s_\theta$ that is trained using the CDSM loss function and reflected reverse SDE equation. To evaluate an input sample $x_T\sim Uniform(\Omega)$, which would need to be generated via a random walk or some other method, the reflected SDE $$dX(t)=-\sigma^2(t)s_\theta(X(t),t)dt+\sigma(t)d\overleftarrow{W}(t)+d\overleftarrow{L}(t),$$where (per the above)$\mu$ is taken to zero everywhere, is solved numerically to generate the final output (as usual).

The authors extend the so called _predictor corrector_ method of applying several steps of Langevin dynamics to their model.

The authors spend some time discussing why Latent Diffusion models do not (according to the authors) require the use of thresholding, differentiating these models from those presented herein in the process. Reflection diffusion models (and any model that works in pixel space) works over a outer bound of the support of the data distribution while latent diffusion models work on a submanifold of the data distribution that is learned via a [[Computer Science/Machine learning/Generative models/Variational autoencoder]].
