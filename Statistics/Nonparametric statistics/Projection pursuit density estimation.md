Projection pursuit density estimation is a method of non-parametric density estimation, and related to [[Parzen-Kernel density estimators]]. As the name suggests projection pursuit methods are based on projecting data in various ways to revel its structure. Projection pursuit tends to be more effective at estimating densities of high dimensional data compared to other methods which product biases results.  [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_pursuit).

Principle component analysis is actually a special case of projection pursuit.


# Introduction
Much of the following is taken from [[@Friedman_1984]] and notes provided by my supervisor(2023) [Yoaliang-Yu](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~y328yu/index.html).

Given finite iid [[Random samples|random sample]] $X_1,X_2,\dots,X_N$ from some common underlying probability distribution. We seek to find a probability density function $p(X)$ that fits the given data; in particular $$p(X)\approx p_t(X)=p_0(X)\prod_{i=1}^t f_i(\theta_i\cdot X),$$where $p_t$ is the density estimate after $t$ iterations of the estimation procedure and $p_0$ is an initial (guess) multivariant density function which is used as the initial model, and should reflect the best [[Bayesian probability theory#^abeae7|prior]] knowledge; $\theta$ is a unit vector specifying a direction in $\mathbb{R}^d$ so that $\theta\cdot X$ is an orthogonal combination (coordinates); and $f_i$ is a non-negative univariate function, in fact due to the previous restriction $f$ is a [[Ridge function]].  

As an initial guess $p_0$ is typically taken to be a [[Families of distributions#Gaussian (Normal) distribution|normal distribution]] with [[Random samples#^52b789|sample mean]] and sample covariance matrix.

The above estimator can be restated in terms of the step-wise recursive formula $$p_t(X) = p_{t-1}(X)\cdot f_{t}(\theta_t\cdot X).$$
Approaching the problem of density estimation in this way allows us to more easily refine our choice for $f_t$ at each step by applying [[Maximum likelihood]] (or more general objective like [[Information theory#Renyi divergence|Renyi-divergence]]); specifically, after fixing $p_{t-1}$ in the previous iteration we proceed by picking parameter values for $\theta_t$ in the maximization $$\max_{\substack{\theta\in S^{d-1}\\ f_t}}\int_{-\infty}^\infty p(x)\log(f_t(\theta_t\cdot x))\,dx$$subject to the constraint $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty p_{t-1}(x)f_t(\theta_t\cdot x)\, dx=1$$which is required to ensure $p_t$ remains a valid [[Distribution functions of random variables#^c46bbe|probability density function]]. 

We can make use of a [[Ridge function#^789e2b|Radon transformation]] to simply the greedy choice procedure for $\theta$ into one that has a closed form solution. In particular, the above becomes $$\max_{\substack{\theta\in S^{d-1}\\ f_t}}\int_{-\infty}^\infty RT(p(x),\theta_t,\tau)\log(f_t(\tau))\,d\tau$$subject to the constraint $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty RT(p_{t-1}(x),\theta_t,\tau)f_t(\tau)\, d\tau=1.$$Fixing the direction $\theta_t$ we can obtain these close form solution of $f_t$,  $$f_t(\tau) = \frac{RT(p(x),\theta_t,\tau)}{RT(p_{t-1}(x),\theta_t,\tau)}$$which can substituted back into the initial equation above to yield the optimization objective $$\max_{\substack{\theta\in S^{d-1}\\ f_t}}\int_{-\infty}^\infty RT(p(x),\theta_t,\tau)\log\left(\frac{RT(p(x),\theta_t,\tau)}{RT(p_{t-1}(x),\theta_t,\tau)}\right)\,d\tau = \mathbb{KL}(RT(p(\tau),\theta_t)|RT(p_{t-1}(\tau),\theta_t)),$$where the last term is the [[Information theory#^26fd09|Kullback-Leibler divergence measure]] between the current estimate and true underlying values in direction $\theta_t$ (in the unimodal sense). In summary, this procedure searches for a direction $\theta_t$ along which the target density $p$ and current estimate $p_{t-1}$ differ the most (in terms of KL-divergence). It is then hoped that the augmentation of $p_{t-1}$ by $p_t$ determined by $\theta_t$ will produce a reduction in the dissimilarity between $p_{t-1}$ and $p$ "proportional" to the divergence criteria that $\theta_t$ is selected from. 

Aside, if we do not assume a form for $f$, e.g., not assumed to be polynomial functions of a specified degree, we could apply a secondary optimization procedure for fitting $f$'s at each step using [[Ridge function|Ridge functions]] by [[Ridge function#Function reconstruction via Tikhonov regularization|approximating the inverse Radon transformation via Tikhonov regularization]]. 

Now as the true underlying density $p$ is not known, the above greedy strategy is not of direct use; however, computing empirical approximates of this value can be used in its place. Moreover, as the objective is both non-convex and non-smooth so direct (naive) maximization over the function domain is not feasible. Instead, we take advantage of univariate transformations, i.e., using Radon transformations of data, to be able to apply univariate density estimations methods to the data. These estimates are then combined through the recursive multiplication procedure to yield the final estimate. In particular, as$$RT(p(\tau),\theta) = \int_{z\in H_\theta^\perp}p(\tau\theta+z)\, dz = \int_{H_\theta}p(x)\, dx$$it follows that $\theta\cdot X_1,\theta\cdot X_2,\dots,\theta\cdot X_N$ is distributed according to $RT(p(\tau),\theta)$. 

With the above in mind, we proceed by selecting a direction $\theta$ and projecting the data as above, where a univariate density estimation procedure is applied to get an approximation,  $f(\theta\cdot \tau)$, of $RT(p(\tau),\theta)$. The univariate density of $RT(p_{t-1}(\tau),\theta)$ in the direction of $\theta$ must be estimated effectively in order to perform previous step. Short of performing the Radon transformation, one method of doing this is to use Monte Carlo sampling on $p_{t-1}$ to generate many iid samples to form a more accurate estimation.
