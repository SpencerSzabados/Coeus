Monte Carlo methods are applied to many different approximation problems e.g., generating [[Random samples]] from some distribution that cannot be sampled using (standard) [[Inverse transform sampling]], the rendering equation used in [[Ray tracing and light transport]], etc.

# Monte Carlo integration 
In the context of [[Bayesian probability theory]] we often require a method of approximating intractable integrals (e.g., expectation of a function with respect to a posterior distribution for model parameters to form a prediction with lowest expected loss); More generally, we are interested in evaluating integrals of the form $$E_\theta[f(x,\theta)] = \int f(x,\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta$$where $p(\theta)$ is a assumed prior.

Such expectations can be estimated by Monte Carlo (above) using average sampling $$E_\theta[f(x,\theta)]\approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N f(x,\theta^{(i)})$$where $\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)},\dots,\theta^{(N)}$ are generated by a process such that $\theta^{(i)}\sim p(\theta)$. Rudimentary methods typically take $\theta^{(i)}$ to be independent; however, when $p(\theta)$ is a complex distribution generating such independent samples is often infeasible. regardless, the above approximation still provides a unbiased estimate of the expectation provided the dependence is not too high between samples.  [[@Neal_1996]].


# Markov chain Monte Carlo
The process of generating the random samples $\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(N)}$ is where must of the difficulty of applying Monte Carlo often arises.

Monte Carlo can be intractably expensive for high-dimensional problems requiring many samples to converge. A method that allows us to do this more efficiently is based around designing and simulating [[Markov chains]] that converge to the desired distribution, where samples $\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)},\dots$ are drawn following a Markov chain process conditioned on the previously drawn sample.

Clearly, the output of these methods is sensitive to the initial state, consequently, these methods are run through several iterations before "valid" samples can begin to be drawn. This is one of the major limitations of Markov chain Monte Carlo, as in some situations it may take a large number of iterations for the Markov process to converge to the desired distribution and it can be difficult to tell exactly when this occurs. This convergence property is referred to as the _mixing time_ for the Markov chain process. 

## Sampling algorithms
[[Markov chains]] show up implicitly in the constructions of many different sampling algorithms used for Monte Carlo simulation. 

### Gibbs sampling 
Gibbs sampling (known as the _heatbath method_ in physics or single component [[Monte Carlo simulation#Metropolis-Hasting algorithm|Metropolis-Hastings]]) is an iterative Markov chain procedure for sampling multi-dimensional variables that follow distributions that don't readily yield to (more) direct sampling methods. In Gibbs sampling latent variables are sampled from conditional distributions given the current (sampled) values of other (previous) variables. See [[@Neal_1996]] and [[@Casella_1992]] for an overview of the algorithm and [[@Roberts_1992]] for a study of convergence.

See [Avdvanced Statistical Computing](https://bookdown.org/rdpeng/advstatcomp/gibbs-sampler.html) and [The Book of Statistical Proofs](https://statproofbook.github.io/P/mvn-cond.html) for derivation of an example of conditional normal sampling.

There are two main forms of Gibbs sampling, the first called _Chromatic Gibbs sampling_ samples conditionally independent latten variables in parallel, and the second dubbed _linear (sequential) Gibbs sampling_ which samples latten variables conditionally one after another from the most recent values. [[@Roberts_1997]]

#### Parallel (Chromatic) Gibbs sampling 
Parallel Gibbs sampling is most applicable if the components of the random vector (i.e., the random vectors being sampled) are poorly coupled (ideally pairwise independent).

**Problem statement: (Chromatic Gibbs sampling)** Suppose we are interested in sampling a multi-dimensional random vector $\theta=(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_d)\sim \pi$, where $\pi$ is the target distribution of interested that can't be directly sampled but can be used to generate random values from its conditionals, and $\theta_i$ are _nearly_ all pairwise independent. Then $\pi$ can be sampled by simulating a Markov process, such as that in the below algorithm:  

```pseudo
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Chromatic Gibbs sampling algorithm}
\begin{algorithmic}
\Input Markov chain $\{\theta^{(t)}\}_{t=1}$ constructed implicitly in terms of the (transitionary) conditional distributions $\pi_i(\theta) = \pi(\theta_i|\theta_1,\dots,\theta_{i-1},\theta_{i+1},\dots,\theta_d)$, along with an initial state $\theta^{(0)}$.
\Output Sequence $\{\theta^{(t)}\}_{t=k,\dots}$ of Markov chain states $\theta^{(t)}=(\theta_1^{(t)},\dots,\theta_d^{(t)})$ following stationary distribution $\pi$.\\

    \State Let $\theta^{(0)}$ be given
    \While{$t=1,2,\dots$}
        \For{$i=1,\dots,d$}
            \State Sample a state $\theta^{(t)}_i\sim \pi_i(\theta)=\pi(\theta_i|\theta_1^{(t-1)},\dots,\theta_{i-1}^{(t-1)},\theta_{i+1}^{(t-1)},\dots,\theta_d^{(t-1)})$
            \State 
        \EndFor  
    \EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
```

Observe the values computed by the inner loop can be computed asynchronously in parallel, hence the name.

### Metropolis-Hasting algorithm 
The Metropolis algorithm is based on the [[Simulated annealing process]] (or vice versa according to [[@Salamon_2002]]) and is a method of generating samples from a (implicitly defined) Markov chain with the desired stationary distribution. See [[@Neal_1996]].

The metropolis algorithm computes state transitions by drawing sample (state transition) $\theta^{(t+1)}$ states using a _proposal distribution_, say $T(s|\theta^{(t)})$, that is responsible for generating new sates given the current state $\theta^{(t)}$, and then either accepting or rejecting this sate based on its probability of occurring with respect to the desired Markov chain invariant (equilibrium) distribution $p$; that is,

```pseudo
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Metropolis algorithm}
\begin{algorithmic}
\Input Markov chain $\{\theta^{(t)}\}_{t=1,\dots}$ constructed implicitly in terms of a transitionary (proposal) distribution $T(s|\theta^{(t)})$ and a desired stationay distribtion $p$.
\Output Sequence $\{\theta^{t}\}_{t=k,\dots}$ of Markov chain states following stationary distribution $p$.
    \While{$t=1,2,\dots$}
        \State Sample a potential state $s\sim T(s|\theta^{(t)})$
        \If{$p(x)\geq p(\theta^{(t)})$}
            \State $\theta^{(t+1)}\gets s$ with probability $p(s)/p(\theta^{(t)})$
        \Else
            \State $\theta^{(t+1)}\gets \theta^{(t)}$ 
        \EndIf    
    \EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
```

The chosen proposal distribution must by symmetrical and satisfy the condition $T(x|\theta)=T(\theta|s)$ in order for this method to converge. 

The distribution $p$ is typically, depending on the context, defined in terms of an _energy function_, i.e., $p\propto e^{-E(\theta)}$. If this formulation is used candidate states with lower energy than the previous are always accepted while states of higher energy are accepted with probability proportional to $\exp\{-(E(s)-E(\theta^{(t)}))\}$. This last formulation is particularly nice, since for any given step the state distributions (i.e., the frequency of generating states) are given exactly as in term of Gibbs distributions (a form of [[s-Concave densities#Log-concave densities|log-concave distribution]]) $$p_t = \frac{e^{-f(x)/\beta_t}}{\int e^{-f(y)/\beta_t}\,dy}$$
