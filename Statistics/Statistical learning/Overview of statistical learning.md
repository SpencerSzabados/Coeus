# The division between statistical learning (modeling) and machine learning 
The two major goals of both [[Statistical learning]] and [[Overview of machine learning|machine learning]] are inference and prediction. Inference focuses on the study and creation of mathematical models that represent the underlying data-generation processes responsible for the creation of the input data. On the other hand, prediction aims to produce reasonable forecasting (foretelling) of yet unobserved outcomes by extrapolated from previously observed events (e.g., identifying the best course of action in medical treatment based on previously observed similar symptoms). 

Both methods from statistics and machine learning can, in principle, be used for both prediction and inference. In application however, statistical methods have historically focused on inference, with a strong emphasis on the creation and fitting of project-specific models, used to compute quantitative measures of confidence for suspected relationships and assumptions. On the other hand, machine learning efforts are primarily aimed at prediction, using general-purpose algorithms to find patterns in large (hopefully representative) data sets, with minimal assumptions about the data-generating mechanisms of the problem. With their utility emphasised when dealing with "wide data", with more input variables than subjects. [[@Bzdok_2018]] [[@Breiman_2001]]

A consequence of machine learnings lack of explicit models and assumptions on data (e.g., lack of inference abilities) solutions can be difficult to directly interpret, at least compared to explicit statistical models. This issue of transparency in how many machine learning methods reach a particular conclusion has spurred research into so called explainable (or human interpretable) machine learning methods, see [[Explainable machine learning and AI]].
